Somewhere between 5% and 10% of a typical home's electricity goes to devices that are nominally *off*. That's the phantom load — also called vampire power or standby draw — and while individual devices sip tiny amounts, a modern home runs dozens of them, around the clock, every day of the year.

## The math that makes phantoms matter

A single watt of continuous draw uses 8.76 kWh per year — about **$1.50/year** at the U.S. average rate. That sounds like nothing. But standby loads stack:

| Device | Typical standby | Cost/year (US avg) |
|---|---|---|
| [Cable/satellite DVR box](/appliance/cable-box/) | 15–25 W | $22–37 |
| [Game console](/appliance/game-console/) (instant-on mode) | 10–15 W | $15–22 |
| [AV receiver](/appliance/av-receiver/) | 2–10 W | $3–15 |
| TV (modern) | 0.5–3 W | $1–4 |
| [Printer](/appliance/printer/) | 2–5 W | $3–7 |
| Microwave clock | 2–4 W | $3–6 |
| Coffee maker with clock | 1–3 W | $1.50–4.50 |
| Chargers left plugged in (each) | 0.1–0.5 W | under $1 |
| Garage door opener | 3–8 W | $4–12 |
| Smart speakers (each) | 2–4 W | $3–6 |

A house with a DVR, two consoles on instant-on, an AV receiver, three smart speakers, and the usual kitchen clocks can easily idle at **80–120 watts** — 700–1,000 kWh and **$120–180 per year** in an average-rate state, doubled in California or New England. Always-on *intentional* devices ([router](/appliance/wifi-router/), [cameras](/appliance/security-camera/), [NAS](/appliance/nas-server/)) sit on top of that.

## Which phantoms to hunt (and which to ignore)

**Worth killing:**

- **Set-top boxes** — the single worst offender per device. If you've switched to streaming, that idle DVR is pure waste; a [streaming stick](/appliance/streaming-stick/) does the job at 5 W *while in use*.
- **Instant-on / rest modes** on consoles — the energy-saver setting typically costs you 10 extra seconds of boot time and saves $15–20/year per console.
- **Entertainment clusters** — TV + receiver + soundbar + console + subwoofer on one power strip, switched off together.
- **Rarely-used devices**: the guest room TV, the garage beer fridge (not a phantom, but the same logic — [it's $5–15/month](/appliance/mini-fridge/)), the printer used monthly.

**Not worth the effort:**

- Modern phone/laptop chargers with nothing attached — fractions of a watt.
- Anything you'd have to re-program or re-pair daily. Convenience has value; a phantom you'll actually re-enable in a week isn't a saving.
- LED clocks. The microwave clock costs $3/year — annoyance-driven optimization ends here.

## The 20-minute audit

1. **Night test**: with everything "off" and the fridge briefly unplugged, check your meter — many digital meters show instantaneous watts. That number is your whole-house floor. Under 50 W is tight; 150+ W means there's a phantom worth finding.
2. **Room-by-room**: anything with a light, clock, or warm power brick draws standby. Note set-top boxes, receivers, subwoofers, and consoles.
3. **Measure the suspects**: a [smart plug with energy monitoring](/guides/how-to-measure-appliance-power/) reveals each device's overnight draw.
4. **Group and switch**: power strips (or smart plugs on schedules) for the entertainment center and desk cluster — the two densest phantom habitats.

## Should you unplug everything?

No — and the full analysis is in [Does Unplugging Actually Save Money?](/guides/does-unplugging-save-money/). The honest summary: kill the handful of 10+ watt offenders with strips and settings once, then stop thinking about it. The real bill-movers are [heating](/guides/space-heater-cost-mistakes/), cooling, and [hot water](/appliance/electric-water-heater/) — phantom hunting is the warm-up, not the workout.
