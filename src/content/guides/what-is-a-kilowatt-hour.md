Your entire electric bill is built on one unit: the **kilowatt-hour (kWh)**. Understand it, and every number on this site — and your bill — becomes obvious.

## Watts measure speed, kilowatt-hours measure amount

A **watt** is a rate — how fast a device consumes energy *right now*, like the speedometer in a car. A **kilowatt-hour** is a quantity — how much energy was actually consumed, like the odometer. One kilowatt-hour is 1,000 watts sustained for one hour.

The two questions that matter for any device:

1. **How many watts does it draw while running?**
2. **How many hours does it actually run?**

Multiply them, divide by 1,000, and you have kWh. Multiply by your rate and you have dollars. That's the whole formula — it's what our [calculator](/calculator/) does with current state rates built in.

## Getting an intuition for 1 kWh

One kilowatt-hour is:

- A [space heater](/appliance/space-heater/) on high for **40 minutes**
- A [55-inch TV](/appliance/tv-55-inch/) for about **12 hours**
- A [refrigerator](/appliance/refrigerator/) for about **7 hours**
- An [LED bulb](/appliance/led-bulb/) for about **110 hours** — four and a half days
- Charging a [phone](/appliance/phone-charger/) roughly **50 times**
- About **3–4 miles** of driving in a typical EV

Notice the pattern: **anything that makes heat is expensive, everything else is cheap**. A device that heats air or water (heater, dryer, oven, water heater) draws 1,000–5,000 watts. Everything else in your house — screens, lights, chargers, fans — mostly draws 5–200 watts. This single fact explains about 80% of every electric bill.

## What a kilowatt-hour costs

The U.S. average residential rate is currently around 17¢/kWh, but the spread is huge — roughly 11¢ in the cheapest states to over 40¢ in Hawaii. Check [your state's current rate](/states/). Note that your *effective* rate (total bill ÷ kWh used) is usually higher than the advertised rate, because bills add fixed charges, delivery fees, and taxes.

## The mental shortcut worth memorizing

Here's the trick that makes you dangerous at parties:

> **At ~17¢/kWh, every continuous 100 watts costs about $12–15 per month.**

A 100 W device running 24/7 uses 73 kWh/month — about $12.50 at the average rate. So:

- A [home server](/appliance/home-server/) idling at 100 W? ~$12/month.
- An old [cable box](/appliance/cable-box/) at 20 W? ~$2.50/month.
- A [hot tub](/appliance/hot-tub/) averaging 200 W? ~$25/month.
- A [space heater](/appliance/space-heater/) that averages 500 W across the day? ~$60/month.

Scale by your own state: in California, roughly double these; in North Dakota, roughly two-thirds.

## Watts vs. volts vs. amps, in one paragraph

Volts × amps = watts. Your outlets supply ~120 volts; a device drawing 12.5 amps is pulling 1,500 watts — the practical maximum for a standard circuit, which is why space heaters, kettles, and hair dryers all cluster at 1,500 W. Big appliances (dryers, EV chargers, water heaters) use 240 V circuits precisely so they can draw more watts without enormous currents.

## Where labels mislead

The wattage printed on a device is its **maximum**, not its average. A "1,200 W" microwave describes cooking power; a "500 W" PC power supply describes capacity, not consumption; a fridge's label amperage implies a draw it only hits when the compressor starts. For realistic *typical* figures, that's exactly what our [appliance database](/appliances/) provides — or [measure the real number yourself](/guides/how-to-measure-appliance-power/) with a $10 plug.
