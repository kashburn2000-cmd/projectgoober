"Gas is cheaper" is folk wisdom with a real basis and important exceptions. Here's the appliance-by-appliance truth, and the simple conversion that lets you compare fuels yourself.

## The conversion that makes fuels comparable

Gas is sold in **therms** (about 29.3 kWh of raw energy each). At a typical residential gas price of $1.20–2.00/therm, raw gas energy costs roughly **4–7¢ per kWh-equivalent** — versus electricity's 11–43¢ per kWh depending on [your state](/states/). Raw gas energy is nearly always cheaper.

But raw energy isn't the whole story. Combustion appliances waste some heat (vented outside), electric resistance converts at ~100%, and heat pumps *multiply* — delivering 2–4 kWh of heat per kWh purchased. Fixture matters too: a gas connection often carries a fixed monthly service charge ($10–30) regardless of use, which quietly changes the math for light users.

## Appliance by appliance

### Water heater

Gas tanks beat electric resistance tanks on running cost almost everywhere — often $150–300/yr for a family. But the [heat pump water heater](/appliance/heat-pump-water-heater/) rewrote this category: it typically beats gas on running cost too, especially in moderate-rate states. Ranking for most homes: **heat pump electric < gas < resistance electric**, with [tankless](/appliance/tankless-water-heater/) variants shaving standby losses within each fuel.

### Clothes dryer

A [gas dryer](/appliance/gas-dryer/) costs roughly half as much per load as a [standard electric dryer](/appliance/electric-dryer/) at average prices — $15–40/yr difference for typical families, more with heavy use. The [heat pump dryer](/appliance/heat-pump-dryer/) again complicates the ranking, matching or beating gas per load without the hookup. If you already have a gas connection in the laundry, gas or heat-pump electric are both sound; buying a resistance electric dryer when alternatives exist is the money-losing pick. [Full comparison](/compare/electric-dryer-vs-gas-dryer/).

### Cooking

The cost gap here is small enough to ignore — cooking is a minor bill line (~$5–15/month either way). A gas burner sends much of its heat around the pan; [induction](/appliance/induction-cooktop/) puts ~85–90% into it, largely erasing gas's raw-fuel advantage. Choose the stove by cooking preference, indoor air quality, and what your kitchen supports; the electricity-vs-gas cost difference won't buy you lunch. ([Induction vs. radiant electric](/compare/induction-cooktop-vs-electric-stove/) is the comparison with actual money in it.)

### Heating

The big one — and the nuanced one. Gas furnaces beat *resistance* electric decisively. Against a modern [heat pump](/appliance/heat-pump/), it's a live contest decided by your gas price, electric rate, and climate: cheap-gas states favor the furnace, high-gas or moderate-electric states favor the heat pump. We walk the full decision in [Heat Pump Economics 101](/guides/heat-pump-economics/).

## The fixed-charge trap

If your *only* gas appliance is a stove — common in condos — you may be paying a $15–25 monthly service charge to fuel $8 of cooking. Dropping the gas connection entirely (induction + electric or heat-pump alternatives at replacement time) saves the fixed charge forever. Conversely, if your home already burns gas for heat *and* water *and* drying, the marginal service cost is spread across enough use to be irrelevant.

## Bottom line by scenario

- **Already have gas everywhere**: keep it for the dryer and furnace until replacement time; at replacement, compare heat-pump options honestly.
- **All-electric home**: don't add a gas connection; upgrade resistance appliances to heat-pump versions — that captures most of gas's advantage without the hookup.
- **Gas stove only**: run the fixed-charge math; you may be paying a subscription fee for a burner.
- **Everyone**: the fuel matters most for the appliances that make lots of heat — heating, hot water, drying. For everything else, the [electric meter's rate](/states/) is what decides.
