Every winter, space heaters produce two contradictory stories: "I saved $80 turning down the furnace" and "my bill went up $200 and I don't know why." Both are real. A [space heater](/appliance/space-heater/) is a tool with sharp edges — here's the math that decides which story you get.

## The baseline number

A standard space heater on high draws 1,500 watts — the practical maximum from a wall outlet. Run 8 hours a day, that's 12 kWh daily, or about 365 kWh a month. At current rates that means roughly **$40/month in a cheap-power state, $60 at the U.S. average, and $115+ in California or New England**. Per heater. ([Your state's exact number is here](/appliance/space-heater/).)

Every 1,500 W heater costs the same to run — the $30 unit and the $150 "ceramic tower with digital thermostat" convert electricity to heat identically. Features buy comfort and safety, not efficiency.

## The four classic mistakes

### Mistake 1: Heating a room nobody's in

The heater in the drafty home office, still running during the two hours of meetings you took from the kitchen. A heater without a schedule or thermostat runs at full draw regardless. Fix: use the built-in thermostat, or a $10 smart plug with a schedule.

### Mistake 2: Supplementing instead of substituting

The savings case for a space heater is **zone heating**: drop the whole-house thermostat several degrees, heat only the occupied room. What actually happens in many homes: the central thermostat stays at 70°F *and* the space heater runs on top. That's strictly additive — you're paying the furnace bill plus $40–115/month.

### Mistake 3: Fighting a heat pump

If your central heat is a [heat pump](/appliance/heat-pump/), it delivers heat at 2–4× the efficiency of a space heater. Displacing heat-pump heat with resistance heat *increases* your bill even when the zone-heating logic feels right. Space heater substitution only wins against resistance heat ([electric furnace](/appliance/electric-furnace/), [baseboards](/appliance/baseboard-heater/)) or genuinely expensive fuels — or when it lets you keep a large house much colder overall.

### Mistake 4: All-night bedroom heating

Eight sleeping hours at 1,500 W is the most expensive comfort in the house — and most safety guidance says don't run portable heaters unattended anyway. An [electric blanket](/appliance/electric-blanket/) delivers sleeping warmth at about **one-tenth the cost**; see the [head-to-head](/compare/space-heater-vs-electric-blanket/).

## Setups that genuinely save money

- **The remote worker**: house at 62°F all day, one heater with a thermostat in the office. The whole-house heating you *don't* buy usually exceeds the heater's cost — this is the textbook win.
- **The one-cold-room house**: rather than raising the whole thermostat because the back bedroom is freezing, fix that room with 750 W on a thermostat.
- **The overnight substitution**: house drops to 60°F at night, electric blankets handle beds. Nearly all the savings, none of the unattended-heater risk.

## Choosing by heater type

[Oil-filled radiators](/appliance/oil-filled-radiator/) are quiet and steady, fan/ceramic units heat fast, [infrared panels](/appliance/infrared-heater/) target people in larger spaces. Cost per hour at the same wattage is identical across all of them — pick by room and noise, not efficiency claims. The low setting (typically 750 W) costs exactly half the high setting, and a thermostat matters more than either.

One rule covers everything: **a space heater saves money only when it lets you buy less heat somewhere else.** If nothing else in the house changed, its cost is pure addition — and now you know exactly what that addition is.
